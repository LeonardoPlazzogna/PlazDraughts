# Measured results

Everything that was **measured** in this project, with the conditions under
which it was measured. Inferences are marked as such and kept apart from the
data.

Every number here is reproducible: next to each one is the tool that produced
it and the parameters used. Where a measurement was **withdrawn or corrected**,
the reason stays written — those corrections are part of the method, not debris
to hide.

The section numbers are stable: the code, [`results/README.md`](../results/README.md)
and the thesis cite them as §N. The document covers measurements up to
14 September 2026. Measurements still in progress when a section was written
are marked as such: **no provisional number entered the tables**.

**Names used throughout.** *Run A* is the definitive 100-cycle run and *run B*
the 200-cycle run, both on the compute machine and both with every correction of
section 5. *The 300-cycle run* and *the 62-cycle run* are earlier runs, made
before those corrections. *The external engine* is Kingsrow Italian 1.19e by Ed
Gilbert, deliberately weakened (§4.1). The data series behind the figures are in
[`results/series/`](../results/series/).

## 1. The game: measured properties of Italian draughts

Measured on the positions generated by self-play, not estimated on paper.

| quantity | value |
|---|---|
| mean legal moves per position | 5.99 – 6.06 |
| "effective" moves in the search target | 2.91 – 2.99 |
| mean entropy of the target | 1.07 – 1.09 nats |
| mean length of a game | 150 – 159 plies |
| share of drawn games | 43% → 48% (grows with the strength of the network) |
| draws by reaching the no-progress limit | ~35% of all games |
| games stopped at the 300-ply cap | 2 – 4% |

**The most characteristic figure**: the entropy of the target corresponds to
about three effective moves out of six legal ones. The search concentrates its
visits on half of the options, and this value **stays the same** between a
62-cycle network and a 300-cycle one — the sharpness of the target does not
improve with training.

**The distribution, not just the mean.** Over 6,498 positions played by a
trained network (mean 5.84, median 6, maximum observed 16;
`results/series/legal_moves_distribution.csv`):

| legal moves | share | | legal moves | share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 9.5% | | 6 | 16.0% |
| 2 | 5.4% | | 7 | 14.9% |
| 3 | 5.8% | | 8 | 13.3% |
| 4 | 9.9% | | 9 | 6.8% |
| 5 | 10.2% | | 10 or more | 8.2% |

It serves two distinct lines of reasoning, and in both the result runs against
intuition.

*On exploration noise.* One would expect mandatory capture to force a sizeable
share of positions, and so the Dirichlet noise — applied at the root, once per
move — to act on few of them. Positions with **a single legal move are 9.5%**:
nine times out of ten there is a choice. The noise therefore works almost
always, and samples with a degenerate target are a modest minority. The rule of
thumb α ≈ 10 / typical moves gives 1.71 against the 1.5 in use: close enough not
to justify a tuning.

*On search batching.* The low branching, which here is a property of the game,
becomes the mechanism of the defect documented further on: gathering dozens of
leaves per batch in a tree where every node has six children forces the search
to visit almost all of it indiscriminately. It is the reason batching costs much
more in this game than in chess.

### Composition of the training positions

On a sample of 60,000 positions from the buffer of a mature run:

| | |
|---|---|
| positions with at least one king | 73.2% |
| with two or more kings | 67.1% |
| with four or more kings | 36.6% |
| mean kings per position | 2.55 |
| mean pieces on the board | 9.5 |
| true endgames (≤ 8 pieces) | 60.5%, of which 98.1% with kings |
| positions from drawn games | 50.3% |

The buffer is dominated by endgames: games are long and their second half has
few pieces. Half of the training positions carry the label "draw".

## 2. Hyperparameter calibration

### 2.1 Exploration constant of the search

Same network on both sides, a single different parameter, 400 games per point.
**The reference is 1.5.**

| value | score | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 1.00 | 0.450 [0.413 – 0.487] | worse |
| **1.50** | reference | — |
| 2.00 | 0.489 [0.454 – 0.524] | indistinguishable |
| 3.00 | 0.475 [0.438 – 0.512] | indistinguishable |

**Null control** (the reference against itself): 0.487 [0.451 – 0.524], a
deviation of 0.013 from the true value. The measurement is not biased.

**Conclusion**: the value in use is correct and the plateau between 1.5 and 3.0
is flat. Telling apart differences below 27 Elo would take thousands of games.

> **Withdrawn measurement.** A first calibration, with the same procedure but
> without diversifying the openings, had given: 0.50→0.310, 0.75→0.409,
> 1.00→0.422, 1.25→0.531, 2.00→0.557 — a monotonically increasing trend that
> suggested raising the constant. It was an artifact: those 400 games contained
> **about 16 distinct games** (§5.1), and the declared intervals were five times
> narrower than the true ones.

### 2.2 Search budget — the scaling curve

As above, reference 400 simulations, 400 games per point.

| simulations | Elo relative to 400 | gain per doubling |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | −303 | — |
| 200 | −131 | +172 |
| 400 | 0 | +131 |
| 800 | +158 | +158 |
| 1600 | +253 | +95 |
| 3200 | +340 | +87 |

**About 150 Elo per doubling up to 800, then about 90.** No saturation within
3200: five doublings cover 643 Elo.

Interpretation: Italian draughts has a small branching factor but deep, forcing
tactics, because of the chains of mandatory captures. At 400 simulations every
root move already has about 70 visits, yet doubling still pays.

*Caution for the figure*: measured on the champion of one specific run; with a
stronger network the slope could differ. At a score of 0.876 (3200 against 400)
one is close to the ceiling that 400 games can resolve.

### 2.3 Discount of the value target

A position *d* plies from the end receives the outcome multiplied by γ to the
power *d*. **Paired** comparison: the same games labelled in two ways, two
candidates from the same starting point, 50 games with identical seeds
(`tools/ab_value_discount.py`).

| | score | back-and-forth king moves per game | length |
|---|---|---|---|
| no discount | 0.910 | 44.2% | 161 plies |
| γ = 0.99 | 0.920 | **30.1%** | **121 plies** |

Paired length difference: **−39.6 plies, standard error 12.5, t = −3.17**.
Discordant outcomes: 8 in favor of the discount, 7 against.

**Behavior improves clearly; strength does not get worse.** Against the external
engine the same candidates score 0.000 and 0.067 — same direction, p = 0.11:
that it translates into strength is **not demonstrated**.

*Side effect to watch*: the discount shrinks the positive labels and leaves the
zeros of draws intact, so the mean signal halves (mean absolute value from 0.49
to 0.26).

### 2.4 Training epochs per cycle

Frozen buffer, candidates from the same starting point, same positions
(`tools/sweep_train.py`).

| epochs | divergence on seen data | on new data | gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 (in use) | 0.1176 | 0.1355 | 0.018 |
| 10 | — | 0.1306 | — |
| 30 | 0.0503 | 0.1308 | 0.081 |

Going from 2 to 30 epochs the model improves by **0.067 on seen data and by
0.005 on new data**: 93% of the extra training is memorization that does not
transfer.

## 3. Diagnosis of learning

### 3.1 Decomposition of the policy loss

The loss decomposes exactly into the entropy of the target plus the divergence
between network and search. The first term is a floor that no training can lower
(`tools/diagnose_targets.py`).

| | 62-cycle run | 300-cycle run |
|---|---|---|
| policy loss | 1.2093 | 1.2204 |
| target entropy | 1.0699 | 1.0942 |
| network–search divergence | 0.1394 | 0.1262 |
| divergence / entropy ratio | 0.130 | 0.115 |
| agreement on the most visited move | 71.0% | 71.4% |

**Several times the cycles, and the picture is almost identical**, the target
included. The system settles on a fixed point: the search produces targets of
the same sharpness, and the network follows them at the same distance.

### 3.2 How much of the target is unpredictable

The target is not a function of the position: at the root the priors are mixed
with random noise, drawn anew at every search. Searching the same position 6
times with different draws, over 150 positions (`tools/target_noise.py`):

| | |
|---|---|
| irreducible floor (with noise) | **0.0098 nats** |
| control, same positions without noise | **0.0000 nats** |
| observed network–search divergence | 0.1262 nats |

**The draw explains 8% of the residual.** The other 92% is learnable signal that
the network does not learn. The control at exactly zero confirms that the
measurement isolates the noise and nothing else.

### 3.3 Calibration of the value head

Positions grouped by predicted value, compared with the real outcome (mean
prediction → mean observed outcome):

| predicted | prediction → observed outcome | positions |
|---|---|---|
| [−1.0 ; −0.6) | −0.953 → −0.988 | 3964 |
| [−0.6 ; −0.3) | −0.440 → −0.591 | 479 |
| [−0.3 ; −0.1) | −0.182 → −0.174 | 711 |
| [−0.1 ; +0.1) | −0.000 → −0.006 | 9237 |
| [+0.1 ; +0.3) | +0.184 → +0.124 | 836 |
| [+0.3 ; +0.6) | +0.443 → +0.498 | 482 |
| [+0.6 ; +1.0) | +0.955 → +0.986 | 4291 |

**Mean calibration gap: 0.024.** Mean absolute error 0.118. The value head is
accurate: when it says it is winning, it wins.

### 3.4 Summary: what limits learning

Ruled out one by one, by measuring:

| hypothesis | measurement that rules it out |
|---|---|
| insufficient search budget | divergence/entropy ratio at 0.115, mid-range |
| insufficient training | 15× the epochs move the new-data figure by 0.005 |
| insufficient network capacity | with 30 epochs it goes down to 0.050 on seen data |
| irreducible noise in the target | the draw accounts for 0.0098 out of 0.126, 8% |

**What remains is the number of distinct positions.** The ceiling on new data
sits at about 0.13 whatever is done with network and training; only new data can
lower it. The replay window covered **3.25 cycles** (200,000 samples against the
61,450 produced by each cycle).

> *Inference, not measurement*: that the ceiling on the divergence causes the
> slowdown in strength is plausible but not demonstrated. The only way to prove
> it is to change the window and see whether the anchors move.

### 3.5 The window was widened: nothing moved

The prediction of §3.4 was put to the test. A run with the replay window raised
from 200,000 to 600,000 samples — **tripled**, and nothing else changed — was
compared cycle by cycle with the reference run over the 62 cycles in common:

| quantity | wide window | reference | difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| total loss | 1.575 | 1.598 | −0.023 |
| policy loss | 1.245 | 1.248 | −0.003 |
| value loss | 0.330 | 0.350 | −0.020 |
| mean value error | 0.302 | 0.318 | −0.016 |
| target entropy | 1.096 | 1.098 | −0.002 |
| plies per game | 159.6 | 153.1 | +6.5 |
| draw share | 0.402 | 0.411 | −0.009 |
| samples in the window | 555,160 | 195,370 | +359,790 |

The only non-negligible differences are on the **value head** (−0.020 in loss,
−0.016 in error) and on the length of the games. The policy head, which is where
§3.4 placed the ceiling, does not move: −0.003.

On the absolute anchors the wide window has three measurement points, against
depth-6 alpha-beta: **0.758 at cycle 20, 0.917 at 40, 0.433 at 60** — against
0.858, 0.883 and 0.833 of the reference at the same cycles. One below, one above,
one far below (§4.6). With three points, one of them anomalous, **there is no
sign that the anchors moved**.

**Two necessary caveats.** The reference run used for this comparison is the one
*before the fix* of defect §5.1 — recognizable by its 43 promotions in 62 cycles,
the figure quoted there — so its arena and promotion columns are not comparable
with the others. The self-play columns are, because they do not go through the
arena. And the comparison covers 62 cycles: it does not rule out a difference
emerging later.

**Consequence for §3.4.** The last hypothesis left standing was the quantity of
distinct positions. Tripling the window did not move it. Either the effect is
smaller than these 62 cycles can resolve, or the slowdown has another cause —
and §4.6 points to a candidate that had not been considered.

## 4. Strength results

### 4.1 Against an external engine

The only measurement of the project that does not go through its own tools.
Opponent: Kingsrow Italian 1.19e by Ed Gilbert, a reference engine for Italian
draughts, deliberately weakened — a hundredth of a second per move, no opening
book, transposition table reduced from 128 MB to 1 MB. 50 games, alternating
colors (`tools/kingsrow.py`).

| champion | won | drawn | lost | score | Elo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 62 cycles, defective arena | 0 | 4 | 46 | 0.040 [0.002–0.078] | −552 |
| 300 cycles, fixed arena | 0 | **19** | 31 | **0.190 [0.123–0.257]** | **−252** |

**+300 Elo on an absolute scale**, with intervals that do not overlap.

Composition: in the old run the 4 draws all ended at −2 in material; in the new
one, **3 of the 19 end level or better**. The losses last as long (median 69
against 71 plies): it does not lose more slowly, it loses less often.

#### The definitive run: 100 cycles against the previous 300

Same protocol, same weakened opponent, 50 games with alternating colors (the
games are in `results/games/`):

| champion | won | drawn | lost | score | Elo |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 62 cycles, defective arena | 0 | 4 | 46 | 0.040 [0.002–0.078] | −552 |
| 300 cycles, fixed arena | 0 | 19 | 31 | 0.190 [0.123–0.257] | −252 |
| **run A, 100 cycles** | 0 | **27** | 23 | **0.270 [0.201–0.339]** | **−173** |
| **run B, 200 cycles** | 0 | **30** | 20 | **0.300 [0.232–0.368]** | **−147** |

Run B adds the fourth row and continues the progression — 4 → 19 → 27 → 30
draws — but **cannot be told apart from A**: +0.030 with standard error 0.049,
p ≈ 0.54. Fifty games do not separate even these two, just as they did not
separate A from the 300-cycle model.

Run A's point estimate exceeds that of the 300-cycle run with a third of the
compute. **The difference is not demonstrated, though**: +0.080 with standard
error 0.050, z = 1.61, **p = 0.107**. At fifty games per side the instrument
does not separate the two, and claiming otherwise would be the same mistake this
document criticizes elsewhere in relative measurements.

**No wins, in any of them.** The progress is entirely in the draws: 4 → 19 → 27.
The model never beats the external reference, it survives more often — and
"survives" is the exact word, not a figure of speech: replaying the games shows
that in more than half of the draws it never gets ahead in material, and in none
of the fifty does it hold a two-piece advantage (§4.5).

By color, over 25 games each (run A): as White 16 draws and 9 losses (0.32), as
Black 11 draws and 14 losses (0.22). The losses are shorter than the draws,
median 125 plies against 170.

#### The caveat weighing on this number

**All 27 draws are closed by the no-progress rule** — none by material, none by
position. And that rule, as §9 documents, **is not the official one**: in the
games examined there, 2 out of 8 would not have been granted by the rules, always
in the same direction, because the implementation closes earlier than the
federation allows.

The divergence rate was therefore **measured again on this sample**, with the
same tool as §9 (`tools/draw_rule_check.py`): twenty-four games at 400
simulations.

| | old network (§9) | **this sample** |
|---|---|---|
| closed by the implemented rule | 8 of 12 | 13 of 24 |
| of these, granted by the official rule too | 6 of 8 | 6 of 13 |
| **NOT granted by the rules** | **2 of 8 (25%)** | **7 of 13 (54%)** |

**The divergence more than doubled.** And in the seven diverging games the
official count stood at 0, 0, 1, 10, 16, 32 and 37 out of forty: in two cases it
had just been reset, that is, the game was not even close to a regulation draw.

The mechanism can be read in the difference between the two criteria. The
implemented one resets only on a capture or a promotion, so **a man move does not
reset it**; the official one counts the king moves of the counted player and
resets at every man move or change of material. Positions with men still on the
board therefore diverge the most — and a stronger model, which keeps the game
alive longer, ends up there more often.

#### Consequence: the comparison between the two runs cannot be decided

Applying to each its own rate, and considering that the games not granted would
have continued with an unknown outcome — between staying draws and becoming
losses, since the model never wins:

| run | draws | rate | corrected score | corrected Elo |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 300 cycles | 19 | 25% | 0.142 – 0.190 | −312 … −252 |
| **run A**, 100 cycles | 27 | **50%** | **0.135 – 0.270** | −323 … −173 |
| **run B**, 200 cycles | 30 | **33%** | **0.200 – 0.300** | −241 … −147 |

> *The rates of the two runs were measured again on the same bench and with the
> same seeds* (24 games each, 24 distinct out of 24, 400 simulations). Run A's
> rate comes out at **50%** against the 54% of the earlier measurement, made with
> a different tool: the two agree, so the historical value was good. The
> difference between A and B — 6 of 12 against 5 of 15 — **is not significant**,
> p = 0.45: over twenty-four games the instrument does not separate the two
> styles.

The intervals still overlap, and run A's **can end below** that of the 300-cycle
run. The apparent advantage of +0.080 — already not significant at p = 0.107 —
does not survive the correction.

**Run B, however, is the least ambiguous case of the three.** It gets more draws
*and* owes a smaller share of them to the house rule, so its band is the
narrowest and the highest: its lower end, 0.200, lies above the midpoint of A. It
still overlaps with A, so no difference is demonstrated here either — but it is
the only one of the three rows whose band does not go below that of the
300-cycle model.

**The honest reading is therefore**: the definitive run gets more draws, but a
larger share of them comes from a house rule the regulations would not grant. It
is not demonstrated that it is stronger than its 300-cycle predecessor, and it is
not ruled out that it is weaker.

The two rates come from small samples (13 and 8 games) and from self-play games,
not games against the external engine: the correction is an order of magnitude,
not a figure. But it is large enough to change the conclusion, and that is why it
must be reported together with the number.

#### The direct comparison resolves the ambiguity: +164 Elo

The two available measurements contradicted each other — alpha-beta put this run
clearly ahead, the external engine found it indistinguishable — and neither could
decide: the first is saturated, the second has almost only draws and losses. A
third way remained, the most direct one: **making the two champions play each
other**.

One hundred games, 400 simulations per side (the configuration both were trained
with), alternating colors, diversified openings (`tools/compare.py`):

| | |
|---|---|
| score of the 100-cycle champion | **0.720 ± 0.028** |
| 95% interval | 0.665 – 0.775 |
| **Elo** | **+164 [+119, +214]** |
| won – drawn – lost | **47 – 50 – 3** |
| distinct games | 94 of 100 |

**Forty-seven wins against three losses.** The 100-cycle run, with the
corrections of section 5, is clearly stronger than the 300-cycle one. The jump
observed against the alpha-beta anchors was therefore real, not an artifact of
saturation.

And the draw rule does not confound this measurement: **it applies to both sides
in the same way**, unlike the comparison with the external engine, where only one
side exploits it. That is why this measurement is cleaner than the other two.

#### Why the external engine had not seen it

The result does not contradict the measurement against Kingsrow, it shows its
**resolution limit**. Against a much stronger opponent both models lose almost
everything, and the difference is compressed into the count of draws — few,
noisy, and dependent on the house rule to boot.

It is the mirror image of saturation, and worth stating as a principle of method:
**a reference loses discriminating power both when it is too weak and when it is
too strong**. Alpha-beta no longer discriminates because we always beat it;
Kingsrow does not discriminate because it always beats us. The yardstick that
discriminates is the one close to one's own level — the frozen generations
(§4.7), or the champion of the previous run, as here.

### 4.2 Learning curve on an absolute scale

Six points of the 300-cycle run, 30 games each, same protocol and same opponent
(the games are in `results/learning_curve/`).

| cycle | draws out of 30 | score | 95% interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (random network) | 0 | **0.000** | ← negative control |
| 50 | 4 | 0.067 | [0.006 – 0.127] |
| 100 | 9 | 0.150 | [0.068 – 0.232] |
| 150 | 5 | 0.083 | [0.017 – 0.150] |
| 200 | 7 | 0.117 | [0.041 – 0.192] |
| 300 | 10 | 0.167 | [0.082 – 0.251] |

Combining the two matches available at cycle 300 (80 games in all):
**0.181 [0.129 – 0.234]** — consistent with the 0.190 measured separately.

**THE SHAPE.** A steep climb up to cycle 100, then flat within the resolution. No
pairwise comparison between points beyond cycle 50 reaches significance:

| comparison | p (exact test) |
|---|---|
| cycle 50 against cycle 100 | 0.21 |
| cycle 50 against cycle 300 | 0.13 |
| cycle 100 against cycle 300 | **1.00** |

Cycle 0 at exactly 0.000 is the negative control of the bench: a network with
random weights gets nothing, so the score measures play and not artifacts.

**Operational consequence**: two hundred cycles beyond the hundredth produce no
gain measurable with this instrument. For the same hours of compute, two
150-cycle runs are worth more than one of 300, because they allow a controlled
comparison instead of a single trajectory.

> *Revised later*: on the corrected runs, doubling the cycles does buy a
> measurable gain, +67 Elo in the direct comparison (§4.8). The conclusion above
> holds for the 300-cycle run and for this instrument.

### 4.3 Against fixed-depth opponents

Fixed-depth alpha-beta search, deterministic and reproducible. Trajectory over
300 cycles:

| cycle | depth 4 | depth 6 | depth 8 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.875 | 0.733 | |
| 40 | 0.933 | 0.908 | |
| 60 | 0.917 | 0.933 | 0.625 |
| 120 | 0.883 | 0.742 | 0.675 |
| 180 | 0.925 | 0.758 | 0.700 |
| 240 | 0.942 | 0.817 | 0.775 |
| 300 | 0.925 | 0.825 | 0.750 |

Converted to Elo, each anchor gives about **+100 points from cycle 20 to 300**.

**Two caveats.** Depth 4 is saturated above 0.9 and no longer discriminates. And
the oscillations are real, not noise: between the maximum (0.933) and the minimum
(0.608) of depth 6 there are almost six standard errors — the network really gets
worse for long stretches.

**Limit of the scale**: the same weakened external engine beats depth 8 four
games to nothing. The whole alpha-beta scale lies far below the state of the art,
and that must be said every time it is used: it says how we change, not where we
stand.

#### The definitive run saturates the whole scale

The 100-cycle run carried out on the compute machine — the first that
incorporates all the corrections of section 5 — gives values of another order
(`results/series/runA_fixed_depth.csv`):

| cycle | depth 4 | depth 6 | depth 8 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 1.000 | 0.867 | **0.917** |
| 40 | 1.000 | **0.983** | **0.967** |
| 60 | 0.975 | **1.000** | **0.983** |
| 80 | 0.992 | **1.000** | **0.967** |
| 100 | 1.000 | **0.983** | **1.000** |

Sixty games for each measurement at depths 4 and 6. The depth-8 column was
measured separately, **thirty games per row**, and is described just below.

#### The depth-8 column, completed outside the run

During the run depth 8 ran on a longer cadence, because it costs many times the
others, and **a single point** of it was left, cycle 60. The frozen generations
stay on disk, however, and can be replayed at will without training anything —
the same technique as §4.6. Five generations, thirty games each, 400
simulations, alternating colors, seed 31337+g equal for all:

| cycle | points | W-D-L | median plies | distinct games |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.917 | 25-5-0 | 89 | 29/30 |
| 40 | 0.967 | 28-2-0 | 78 | 28/30 |
| 60 | 0.983 | 29-1-0 | 104 | 30/30 |
| 80 | 0.967 | 28-2-0 | 84 | 30/30 |
| 100 | **1.000** | 30-0-0 | 83 | 30/30 |

**Two checks, both passed.** Cycle 100 gave 1.000 again with 30-0-0, identical to
the independent measurement of §4.5 made days earlier. And cycle 60 gives 0.983
over thirty games where the measurement *inside the run*, over twenty, had given
0.975: the offline replay agrees with what the run had recorded live. The second
check also validates the method of replaying the frozen generations, on which
§4.6 and §4.7 rest.

**There are no losses**: zero out of a hundred and fifty games. All the
information lies in the draws, so the rows must be compared on their number, with
the exact test:

| comparison | draws | p |
|---|---|---|
| cycle 20 against cycle 100 | 5 against 0 | **0.052** |
| cycle 40 against cycle 100 | 2 against 0 | 0.49 |
| cycle 60 against cycle 100 | 1 against 0 | 1.00 |
| cycle 80 against cycle 100 | 2 against 0 | 0.49 |

**The precise reading is not "saturated" but almost.** No pair is separated, yet
cycle 20 against 100 sits right on the edge, p = 0.052: depth 8 keeps a **trace**
of discriminating power at the low end — cycle 20 draws five games out of thirty
where the champion draws none — and does not order cycles 40 to 100 in any way.

It is one nuance more than depths 4 and 6, which at cycle 20 were already at 1.00
and 0.87, but not enough to make it an instrument. **It strengthens the
conclusion instead of complicating it**: the deepest opponent the work could
afford holds out a little longer than the others, then gives way in the same
manner.

> *Note*: the cycle-60 row of the depth-8 column is **0.983 over thirty games**,

| 160 | 0.917 | 25-5-0 | 96 | 29/30 |
| 180 | 1.000 | 30-0-0 | 76 | 29/30 |
| 200 | 0.983 | 29-1-0 | 68 | 29/30 |

**Three checks, all passed.** The run had measured depth 8 by itself at cycles
60, 120 and 180, over twenty games: 0.925 · 1.000 · 0.975 against our 0.967 ·
0.967 · 1.000. The differences all lie within sampling error. A distinction from
run A's checks must be made, though: there the champion had been replayed **with
the same seeds**, so exact reproduction was expected and obtained; here the
internal measurements used their own seeds, so only agreement within noise is
expected, and that is what is seen.

**Unlike run A, this column separates.** Two-sided permutation test over two
hundred thousand shuffles:

| comparison | scores | p |
|---|---|---|
| cycle 20 against cycle 200 | 0.800 – 0.983 | **0.0024** |
| cycle 20 against cycle 60 | 0.800 – 0.967 | **0.0089** |
| cycle 40 against cycle 200 | 0.867 – 0.983 | **0.026** |
| cycle 40 against cycle 60 | 0.867 – 0.967 | 0.081 |
| cycle 60 against cycle 200 | 0.967 – 0.983 | 1.000 |

**Depth 8 measures run B's progress up to about cycle 60, and from there on
saturates.** In run A it measured nothing from cycle 20 already. The instrument
is identical: what changes are the two runs.

#### The direct comparison of the two runs on the same anchor

It is the first absolute measurement that compares them with the same opponent,
protocol, number of games and seeds:

| cycle | run A | run B | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.917 | 0.800 | 0.111 |
| 40 | 0.967 | 0.867 | 0.079 |
| 60 | 0.983 | 0.967 | 1.000 |
| 80 | 0.967 | 1.000 | 0.490 |
| 100 | 1.000 | 0.950 | 0.238 |

**No row is significant on its own**, and that must be said first. The two
comparisons in the unsaturated stretch point in the same direction, however, and
combined with Fisher's method they give **p = 0.050**: exactly on the threshold,
that is, a hint and not a proof.

The behavioral evidence is sharper than the score: at cycle 20 run B takes **169
median plies** to finish against depth 8, run A takes **89**. Almost twice as
many, against the same opponent — the profile of a player that wins but cannot
finish.

> **Depth 6, however, does not confirm, and that must be said.** At the same
> cycles the depth-6 column gives 0.867 and 0.983 for A against 0.975 and 0.900
> for B: at cycle 20 it is **B** that does better, at 40 A. No direction. The
> hint that B starts slower therefore comes from **one column out of two**, and
> must be presented as such: depth 8 suggests it, depth 6 does not corroborate
> it. The plies remain the most solid evidence, because they do not depend on the
> score.

> *Reading, to be kept apart from the data*: it is the expected consequence of
> the learning-rate schedule. Run B spreads the same cosine decay over two
> hundred cycles instead of a hundred, so at cycle 20 it is much further behind
> in **its own** schedule than A is at its cycle 20. The two are not the same
> trajectory observed at different moments. It is the "one setting, two
> respects" caveat of §4.8 — argued so far, and now measured for the first time.

#### Handicapping our side instead of strengthening the opponent

If the reference is too weak, the gap can be narrowed from either side.
Increasing the depth does not work (see further below). The other way is to
**take search away from ourselves**: same depth 8, but one hundred simulations
per move instead of four hundred. It costs less, not more, and leaves the anchor
fixed.

Run A, same thirty games and same seeds as the 400 column:

| cycle | 400 sims | **100 sims** | W-D-L at 100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 0.917 | **0.467** | 10-8-12 |
| 40 | 0.967 | 0.900 | 25-4-1 |
| 60 | 0.983 | 0.850 | 22-7-1 |
| 80 | 0.967 | 0.767 | 18-10-2 |
| 100 | 1.000 | 0.917 | 25-5-0 |

**The range grows from 0.083 to 0.450**: more than five times. The instrument has
recovered dynamic range, and the proof that it really has is that cycle 20
separates from all the rest overwhelmingly (p < 0.0001 against cycle 100 and
against 40) — at one hundred simulations that model **loses** against depth 8,
twelve losses against ten wins.

**And here comes the point.** With five times the dynamic range, the instrument
**still does not order cycles 40 to 100**: the best comparison among them is 80
against 100 at p = 0.050, and 40 against 100 gives p = 1.000.

This transforms the statement of §4.3. Before, one could only say "the instrument
is saturated, so we do not know"; now one can say **"an instrument with five
times the resolution was built, verified to be able to separate, and it finds no
ordering after cycle 40"**. The flatness is a property of the models, not of the
measuring bench.

**A second result, on the relationship between network and search.** Removing
three quarters of the simulations, the cycle-20 model loses **0.450** in score;
the cycle-100 one loses **0.083**. The young model depends on search
incomparably more. Between cycles 40 and 100, however, the drops do not line up
(0.067 · 0.133 · 0.200 · 0.083), so the data support the initial jump and **not**
a continuous trend: it must be reported as such.

#### The four columns compared: where there is signal and where there is not

With four columns — two runs times two search budgets — the right question can
be asked once, **without multiple comparisons**: do the generations of a column
all have the same strength, or not? Permutation test on the dispersion of the
group means, a hundred thousand shuffles:

| column | generations | mean | range | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A at 400 sims | 5 | 0.967 | 0.083 | **0.148** — compatible with chance |
| A at 100 sims | 5 | 0.780 | 0.450 | **< 0.00001** |
| B at 400 sims | 10 | 0.943 | 0.200 | **0.00002** |
| B at 100 sims | 10 | 0.760 | 0.300 | **0.0002** |

**Only one column out of four is really saturated: run A at 400 simulations.** It
is exactly the one on which an earlier write-up based the statement that every
fixed opponent had stopped measuring. The statement must therefore be narrowed:
not "fixed-depth opponents no longer discriminate", but "they no longer
discriminate *this* run at *this* search budget".

#### Run B at one hundred simulations: real progress, non-monotonic path

| cycle | 20 | 40 | 60 | 80 | 100 | 120 | 140 | 160 | 180 | 200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| points | 0.617 | 0.617 | 0.783 | 0.867 | 0.667 | 0.917 | 0.683 | 0.700 | 0.883 | 0.867 |

**The net progress is there**: the two initial cycles against the two final ones
give 0.617 against 0.875, p = 0.0001. **The path, however, is not a climb**: the
dispersion is 3.4 times what sampling alone explains, and the sequence
oscillates — 0.667 at cycle 100, 0.917 at 120, 0.683 at 140.

*Mandatory caution*: the dispersion test shows that **some** differences are
real, not that any given pair is. Over ten rows there are forty-five possible
comparisons, and with the Bonferroni correction no single oscillation survives
(the best, 100 against 120, is at p = 0.004 against a threshold of 0.001). It is
the **profile** that carries the weight, not the single comparison — the same
reading §4.7 applies to run A's ladder.

This extends §4.6 from a documented episode to a systematic behavior: the
trajectory oscillates for long stretches, and only the comparison between the
ends shows the gain. It is the profile of a random walk with drift, which is
precisely what the promotion criterion can guarantee.

> *Side note*: at cycle 120 the column shows **23 distinct games out of 30**, the
> lowest of all four columns. It is the same secondary clue that had accompanied
> the cycle-60 anomaly in §4.6 — more concentrated play, fewer moves of equal
> value to draw from. To keep an eye on, not to conclude from: one row out of
> thirty.

#### Why the scale stops at eight: depth has stopped buying difficulty

The choice not to go beyond depth 8 was so far motivated by cost alone. That is
no longer needed: the data say it **would not help**.

| | mean depth 6 | mean depth 8 | difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| run A (5 points) | 0.967 | 0.967 | **+0.000** |
| run B (10 and 3 points) | 0.970 | 0.967 | **−0.003** |
| *300-cycle run* (7 and 5 points) | *0.817* | *0.705* | *−0.112* |

In the two current runs **two more plies of search produce an opponent
indistinguishable from the previous one**. At cycle 20 of run A depth 8 is even
easier than depth 6 (0.917 against 0.867), and at cycle 100 too (1.000 against
0.983). It is not masked noise: the coincidence holds over fifteen points and two
independent runs.

**The third row shows that it was not always so.** In the old 300-cycle run depth
8 was really harder than depth 6, by eleven percentage points. It stopped being
so when the model became strong enough to beat both regardless of depth.

**The mechanism, and the consequence.** The limit of that family of opponents is
not the horizon but the **evaluation function**: searching deeper with a weak
static evaluation does not produce a proportionally stronger player, because the
search merely propagates to the root judgments that stay wrong. The external
evidence agrees — the reference engine, weakened as it is, beats depth 8 four to
nothing.

It follows that **increasing the depth cannot restore discriminating power**, and
depth 10 — which would cost about 6.6 times depth 8, that is three minutes per
game instead of forty-five seconds — would confirm a result that three data
series already predict.

> *The alternative route*: if the gap is too wide, it can be narrowed from the
> other side, **handicapping our player** instead of strengthening the opponent —
> same depth 8, but one hundred simulations per move instead of four hundred. It
> costs **less**, not more, leaves the anchor fixed and deterministic, and either
> discriminates or shows that the gap cannot be bridged anyway. It is the logic
> of the batching measurement of §6 applied in reverse: building a comparison
> that is deliberately asymmetric in the suspect aspect, instead of hoping that a
> symmetric one will reveal it. This measurement was made: see "Handicapping our
> side" above.

**Methodological consequence, to be recorded before the numbers**: an earlier
version of this document named depth 8 as *the only anchor almost certainly not
saturated*. It no longer is. From cycle 40 on, the whole alpha-beta scale says
only "wins by a mile", not by how much — and the three depth-6 rows at 0.983,
1.000 and 0.983 cannot be told apart in any useful way.

This is not a result about the player but about the instrument: **the trajectory
of this run is not measurable with fixed-depth opponents**, and any statement
about its progress beyond cycle 40 must rest on something else. The two relative
measurements left — arena and generation ladder — are exactly those that section
4.6 shows can contradict each other.

The comparison with the 300-cycle trajectory above must therefore be read with
caution: the two runs differ by many corrections besides their length, so the
jump from 0.73–0.93 to 0.98–1.00 cannot be attributed to a single cause. That the
jump is real is hard to deny, though: depth 8, which in the long run never
exceeded 0.775, here scores 0.975 at cycle 60 in the run's own measurement.

### 4.4 Analysis of playing behavior

#### Game length is the only signal that never saturates

Mean length of the self-play games, in blocks of twenty cycles
(`results/series/runA_game_shape.csv`, `runB_game_shape.csv`):

| | 1-20 | 21-40 | 41-60 | 61-80 | 81-100 | 101-120 | 121-140 | 141-160 | 161-180 | 181-200 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| run A | 144 | 150 | 153 | 155 | 157 | | | | | |
| run B | 142 | 152 | 153 | 155 | 156 | 158 | 160 | 161 | 161 | 162 |

**Neither of the two flattens.** From cycle 40 on, run A grows by **+11.3 plies
every hundred cycles** and B by **+6.5**: B ends higher — 169 plies at its last
cycle against A's 159 — because **it lasts twice as long**, not because it grows
faster.

> *Correction of an earlier reading*: looking only at the final values it had
> been concluded that A stabilized after cycle 40 while B kept climbing. The
> slopes say the opposite, and it is A that has the steeper one.

**Why it matters.** It is the only indicator in this collection that does not
saturate: the scores against fixed depths, the generation ladder and the loss all
flatten, this does not. And it rises through stretches in which strength does
**not** grow: §4.7 establishes that run A gains nothing measurable after cycle 50,
while its games keep getting longer up to the hundredth.

It must therefore be treated as **one more misleading signal**, next to those of
§5: it moves in the right direction and says nothing about strength. Whoever used
it as an indicator of progress — the natural temptation, because it is free and
always moves — would be measuring something else.

#### Indicators for both sides

Over 50 games against the external engine, **every indicator computed for both

a characteristic of the game.

### 4.5 The mechanism of the endgame defect

In a position with four kings against one, with the network to move and 800
simulations:

- all **15** legal moves valued **+1.000**
- visits spread almost evenly among them (a uniform spread would give 6.7% each)
- game ended in a **draw** by the no-progress rule

> *On the recorded figures*: the original notes gave the visit shares as ranging
> from 7.4% to 9.4%. That cannot hold for all fifteen moves — fifteen shares of
> at least 7.4% add up to more than 100% — so either the range covered only some
> of the moves or the move count is off. The measurement itself was not kept, so
> the range is left out here and should not be cited.

The value head is **saturated**: when every move wins, the search has no
criterion to choose one. It is the visible limiting case of the phenomenon that in
real play accounts for 63.6% of the king moves in the endgame.

**A note of balance**: the external engine too, with its endgame databases
disabled, does not convert a two-point advantage within the limit. Part of the
phenomenon belongs to the game — non-flying kings, genuinely difficult endgames —
and not only to the method. Separating the two components requires the endgame
databases.

#### How much it really weighs: in real games the advantage never arrives

The defect described above is real but was observed on a **constructed** position,
with four kings against one. It remains to ask how much it counts in the games
the system really plays, and the answer shrinks its reach a great deal.

The fifty games of the definitive run against the external engine were replayed
move by move, following the material balance (`tools/quiet_material.py` on
`results/games/kingsrow_run_a.txt`). A caveat of method that changes the result:
**material must be counted only in quiet positions**, those in which the side to
move has no capture available. With mandatory capture the count oscillates inside
every exchange, and measuring at every ply shows the system going ahead in all
fifty games — an artifact, not a fact.

Counting quiet positions only, with a median of 138 per game:

| | draws (27) | losses (23) |
|---|---|---|
| peak balance reached, median | **0** | +1 |
| games in which it is **never** ahead | **15 of 27** | 11 of 23 |
| lasting advantage of at least 1 piece | 5 of 27 | 1 of 23 |
| **lasting advantage of at least 2 pieces** | **0 of 27** | **0 of 23** |
| balance at the end of the game, median | 0 | −2 |

*Lasting* means held for at least ten consecutive quiet positions. A king counts
as one piece: with the king worth three, the figures do not match.

**The system does not waste won games: it does not reach them.** In more than
half of the draws it is never ahead by even one piece, and in **none** of the
fifty games does it hold a two-piece advantage. The draws are not failed
conversions: they are games held level and closed by the no-progress limit.

This corrects the reading of §4.1, where the progress between runs — four draws,
then nineteen, then twenty-seven — could suggest a player getting closer to
winning. What improved is the ability to **survive**: to hold material against a
stronger opponent. It is real progress, but of a different nature, and must be
stated as such.

**The conclusion must, however, be conditioned on the opponent**, and the
conditioning is the interesting part. The system converts very well when facing
someone within its reach:

| context | outcome |
|---|---|
| self-play, last 20 cycles | 4,583 decisive games out of 8,000, **57%** |
| against depth 6, cycle 100 | 0.983 over 60 games: about 58 wins and 2 draws |
| against depth 8, cycle 60 | 0.975 over 20 games: about 19 wins |

The ability to build an advantage and bring it home is therefore there, and it is
not marginal. What is missing against the external engine is not the **conversion
technique** but the **opportunity**: against a much stronger opponent the
advantage does not arise, and the endgame defect never reaches the point where it
could show.

#### The direct check: against depth eight it always converts

The hypothesis was put to the test where the advantage really arises. The final
champion of run A against alpha-beta at depth eight — the strongest fixed opponent
available — thirty games with alternating colors, 400 simulations, with the same
count of material in quiet positions (`results/series/champion_vs_ab8.csv`):

| | against the external engine | against depth 8 |
|---|---|---|
| score | 0.270 | **1.000** (30 wins out of 30) |
| peak quiet balance, median | 0 | **+4** |
| games in which it is never ahead | 15 of 27 draws | **0 of 30** |
| lasting advantage of ≥2 pieces | **0 of 50** | **19 of 30** |

The two opponents put the system in two opposite regimes. Against the external
engine it never gets ahead; against depth eight it builds an advantage in every
game, keeps a substantial one in two cases out of three, and wastes none.

**The question of conversion is therefore closed in the negative**: no conversion
defect explains the results. Thirty games without a single missed win put that
kind of error at zero in this sample, with a 95% upper bound of about ten percent
(three events out of thirty), and the two draws out of sixty observed against
depth six during the run are compatible with noise.

**Consequence for this section**: the defect described at the start is real as a
phenomenon — the value head saturates, the search loses its criterion of choice —
but it has no practical reach at the playing levels observed. Against opponents
within the system's reach the advantage gets converted; against much stronger
opponents it does not arise. It remains a well-isolated limiting case, and must be
presented as such instead of as an explanation of the results.

**A note on saturation**: that the final champion beats, thirty times out of
thirty, the opponent §4.3 already flagged as exhausted confirms that reading
conclusively. Depth eight is no longer an anchor: it is an opponent the system
always beats.

**Two limits of the measurement.** Material is not position: one can be winning
without being ahead in pieces, and establishing that would require having the
positions evaluated by an external engine. And in the lost games the peak quiet
balance has a median of +1 against the 0 of the draws, which would suggest that
seeking a material advantage is counterproductive — but with these numbers it is
a suggestion, not a result.

### 4.6 When the three measurements contradict each other: the case of cycle 60

**It is the most important result of this collection**, because it is not about
how strong the player is but about whether the method can notice.

At one cycle of the run with the wide window (§3.5), three instruments said three
incompatible things about the **same** model:

| instrument | verdict | nature of the measurement |
|---|---|---|
| arena against the current champion | 0.590 over 144 games → **promoted** | relative |
| generation ladder | **+819, the maximum of the whole ladder** | relative, chained |
| depth-6 alpha-beta | **0.433** over 60 games | absolute |

The first two agree with each other and are both relative: they look into the
same mirror. The third is the only one anchored to an opponent that does not
change.

**The contradiction was verified by replaying the games.** Three neighboring
generations, same starting positions, same opponent, only the network changes:

| generation | against depth 6 | distinct games |
|---|---|---|
| cycle 50 | 0.850 | 20 of 20 |
| **cycle 60** | **0.475** | 17 of 20 |
| cycle 70 | 0.725 | 20 of 20 |

Between cycle 50 and cycle 60: difference 0.375, z = 2.7, p ≈ 0.006. **The drop
belongs to the model, not to the measurement**, and at cycle 70 the trajectory
recovers by itself.

Two side findings. The cycle-60 model produces **17 distinct games out of 20**
against the 20 out of 20 of its neighbors: it plays in a more concentrated way, so
it leaves the opponent fewer moves of equal value to draw from. And the only
column of that cycle that falls outside two standard deviations with respect to
cycles 41-69 goes in the same direction: the **target entropy, 1.033 against a
mean of 1.073, z = −3.54**. All the others — draws, plies, captures, promotions
to king, truncated games, arena score — are within the norm.

**What follows from it, and what does not.** The measured fact is that a promotion
decided by a relative criterion can make the champion worse in absolute terms,
and that the generation ladder not only fails to notice, but in this case recorded
its own maximum exactly there. The promotion criterion only guarantees that the
candidate beats *that* champion, and with scores that in the run cluster just
above parity (mean 0.574) each single step carries very little evidence.

> *Inference, not measurement*: that this explains the plateau of §4.2 — two
> hundred cycles beyond the hundredth with no measurable gain — is consistent
> with the observation, because a chain of steps each just above parity resembles
> a walk more than a climb. But it is a documented episode, not a demonstration:
> the absolute anchors would need to be measured much more often to know how often
> it happens.

#### The remedy implemented, still to be put to the test

The defect is structural: the arena compares the candidate with the *current*
champion, so the yardstick moves together with the player. The remedy is a
yardstick that does not move — the candidate must also hold up against a
**frozen** generation, taken some tens of cycles back. A random walk often beats
its own previous step, but does not keep beating a fixed reference.

The safeguard is implemented and verified in its operation (`DAMA_ANTIDRIFT`):
when the arena approves, a second comparison is played against the chosen
generation, and the promotion is refused if the candidate regresses. It costs
games only in the cycles in which a promotion would have happened.

**It is off by default, and the reason is not technical.** The two runs of the
comparison planned at the time had to differ by a single parameter; turning the
safeguard on in one of them would have changed two, and the comparison would no
longer say anything. It has to be turned on in a **dedicated third arm**, which is
also the only way to know whether it helps: the question to answer is how many
promotions it blocks and whether the curve against the fixed anchors is affected.

#### The complete series: how often it happens

The ordinary measurement against fixed opponents runs every twenty cycles, so only
**three points** existed for that run. But the champion is frozen every ten cycles
for the generation ladder, and those models stay on disk: they can be measured
again at any time, **without training anything** (`tools/anchors.py`). Eight
generations, twenty games each against depth 6, the same starting positions for
all:

| cycle | score | distinct games | plies |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **0.000** | 19 of 20 | 61 |
| 10 | 0.725 | 20 of 20 | 138 |
| 20 | 0.875 | 20 of 20 | 151 |
| 30 | 0.825 | 20 of 20 | 174 |
| 40 | 0.925 | 20 of 20 | 147 |
| 50 | 0.850 | 20 of 20 | 145 |
| **60** | **0.475** | **17 of 20** | **110** |
| 70 | 0.725 | 20 of 20 | 181 |

Cycle 0 serves as a **negative control**: the initial network gets nothing, so the
bench measures play and not artifacts.

**Cycle 60 is isolated.** The other six trained generations lie between 0.725 and
0.925, with mean 0.821 and standard deviation 0.074: cycle 60 is **4.7 standard
deviations** below. One regression over eight sampled points.

**But the frequency stays undetermined, and that must be said.** Eight points ten
cycles apart observe eight states of the champion out of forty-six promotions,
and a regression that arises and recovers in less than ten cycles — as this one
did exactly — would be invisible. The confidence interval on "one out of eight"
goes from 2% to 47%: not enough to say that it is rare, nor that it is frequent.

It must be read together with §4.3, however, which on the long run already
documents oscillations of the same sign: depth 6 between 0.933 and **0.608**, with
the remark that the network really gets worse for long stretches. The phenomenon
was therefore **already in the data**; what §4.6 adds is not its existence but the
**mechanism**, that is, that a promotion can be accepted precisely while absolute
strength goes down, with both relative measurements signalling the opposite.

#### A second result: the ordinary anchor is saturated

From cycle 20 on the series does not climb — 0.875, 0.825, 0.925, 0.850, 0.725 —
and cycle 70 stands where cycle 10 stood. No pair of points beyond cycle 20 can be
told apart: between the maximum (0.925 at cycle 40) and cycle 70 there are z = 1.7
and p = 0.08.

Before reading it as "it no longer learns" there is the competing explanation, and
it is the right one to prefer: **at 0.83–0.93 that depth is close to the
ceiling**, and twenty games do not tell 0.88 from 0.93. §4.3 already flags this for
depth 4 above 0.9; depth 6 gets there shortly after.

It is a limit of method worth writing down: **for much of the run the ordinary
measuring instrument cannot show progress even when there is some**. The only
anchor still believed able to discriminate was depth 8, which however costs 6.6
times depth 6 and runs once every sixty cycles.

**That one fell too.** The definitive run takes it to **0.975** at cycle 60 (0.983
in the replay of §4.3), against the maximum of 0.775 reached in three hundred
cycles by the previous run. The whole alpha-beta scale is therefore exhausted for
the current models, and measuring progress now requires **the external engine**,
which remains the only unsaturated absolute reference — or anchors built on
purpose, for example frozen generations of the same run.

### 4.7 The champion against its own past: the climb stops halfway

With the fixed anchors saturated, one yardstick remains that can be built from the
saved files alone: the generations frozen every ten cycles. They are relative
measurements, but with a property the promotion arena lacks — **the references do
not move**. The final champion of run A (cycle 100) plays against each of them,
twenty-four games up to cycle 50 and forty-eight after, where resolution is needed.

The measurement was made first at 200 simulations per side and then **repeated in
full at 400**, the configuration of the run. Both are reported, because the
reading was built on the first; the 400 series, below, is the one in
`results/series/runA_vs_generations.csv`.

First measurement, 200 simulations per side:

#### The internal ladder says the opposite, and by a lot

**Operational consequence.** The last forty cycles — twelve hours out of

games spent in comparisons that do not decide (§6, Performance), the evidence
converges: **the limit of this pipeline is neither the data nor the search, it is
the promotion criterion**. And it is the only one of the historical suspects that
none of the scheduled runs puts to the test.

**One caveat.** The loss against gen 9 might mean that that generation is a lucky

## 5. Measurement defects found and fixed

**This section is material on method, not a list of errors.** Each entry is an
instrument that seemed to work and was measuring something else.

On how they were found there is a figure worth as much as the defects themselves:
the first six all emerged from **a number that did not add up**, never from a
code review. The seventh, the only one still latent, emerged in a different way —
from a comparison with the twin implementation written for chess — and precisely
for this reason it is the most instructive: no number could have flagged it
before it did damage, because its effect is to **produce no number at all**.

### 5.1 The arena counted repetitions as independent trials

Without noise at the root and with deterministic networks, **two games that start
the same are the same game**. The only variety came from the first four sampled
plies.

Distinct games out of 400, as the number of sampled plies varies (measured with a
uniform-prior evaluator, hence an optimistic upper bound):

| sampled plies | distinct games |
|---|---|
| 0 | **1** |
| 2 | 49 |
| 4 | 315 |
| 8 | 400 |

With a trained network, which concentrates its visits, the same threshold yields
much less: **329 distinct out of 400 with 10 plies** were measured, from which one
works back to about **2.0 effective moves per ply** and hence to **~16 distinct
games with 4 plies**.

**Consequences.** The confidence intervals were too narrow by a factor of 5 (±24
Elo declared against ±120 real). The sequential promotion criterion computed the
evidence over 400 games when it had sixteen, and promoted almost always: **43
promotions in 62 cycles (69%)**, against **54%** after the fix — and with a
completely different strength profile.

**Fix**: sampled plies raised from 4 to 10, plus the count of distinct games
reported by the engine and an automatic warning when it drops below half.

**The same suspicion about the strength measurement, checked and rejected.** The
measurement against fixed-depth opponents (§4.3) seems to have the same problem:
the champion plays deterministically — search without root noise, then the most
visited move — so two games with the same color can differ only in how the
opponent plays, and the only source of variety is the random tie-break between
moves of equal value. Counting the move sequences over a round of sixty games:
**60 distinct out of 60**, thirty out of thirty for each color. The tie-break is
enough on its own, and the intervals of this measurement can be taken at face
value.

It is a negative result, and for that reason it must be written down: without it,
the same suspicion would come back at every unexpected number.

### 5.2 The value head ignored everything but the sign

The win/draw/loss class was derived from the sign of the label alone. Every
intermediate label was rounded **silently**: no error, no warning, a loss that went
down normally.

It was discovered because two networks trained with and without the value discount
came out **bit-for-bit identical**, while both differed from their starting point.

**Fix**: the label is turned into a distribution over the three classes whose
expected value equals the label itself. Verified inert with the discount off: a
difference of 0.00e+00 over 2000 cases with respect to the previous formulation.

### 5.3 Inverted classes in the evaluation of the candidates

In the comparison tool (`tools/sweep_train.py`), the class was built with the
opposite sign. The consequence was misleading in the worst way: **the more the
model learned, the worse it looked** (4.0 → 5.4 → 6.4 as the epochs grew), and it
would have been read as overfitting.

The signal was there: pure chance over three classes is worth 1.10, and a model
cannot be four times worse than chance on data it has just seen. Verified with an
oracle that guesses everything: 7.2 with the old mapping, 0.0 with the new one.

### 5.4 Elo suppressed on a zero score

The tool refused to estimate the Elo as soon as there were no wins. But 4 draws
out of 50 make 0.040, which is measurable: −552 Elo with interval [−1079, −429].
The formula diverges at 0 and at 1, and only there.

### 5.5 A test bench blind to the phenomenon it had to measure

The first comparison on the value discount had concluded "no effect". Two
independent defects, both of design (`tools/ab_value_discount.py`):

- **the patient was healthy**: the candidates came from a short retraining and did
  not show the defect (0% of wasted moves);
- **the bench was blind**: it measured on already won endgames played by the
  network against itself. Checked later on the champion that does have the defect:
  that bench gives **0.0% over 1562 king moves**, while the same champion against a
  real opponent wastes **56–59%** of them.

Redone with the right patient and the right bench, the effect can be measured
(§2.3).

### 5.6 A recurring error: means over different sets

It appeared **three times**, in different forms, always inverting the sign of the
result:

- mean lengths of converted endgames, computed over different sets of positions
  (the ones each candidate managed to close);
- the share of wasted moves pooling all the moves instead of averaging per game —
  a 250-ply game weighed as much as three of 80, and the sign flipped (49.7%
  against 55.7% pooled, 44.2% against 30.1% per game);
- material advantage read on single frames instead of on sustained stretches.

**The remedy is always the same: pair the measurements.**

### 5.7 A fault that leaves no trace: the arena outcome read and lost

**A latent defect, found before it did damage.** It altered none of the
measurements in this document; it is here because the *way* it fails is material
on method.

The conductor reads the outcome of each comparison from the text the engine
prints. That text was decoded with the operating system's default encoding, which
on one of the two machines used is not the universal one: a single character
outside its repertoire — an accent in a message, or the scrambled bytes of a dying
process — is enough for the output to become unreadable.

The part that matters is **how** it would show. The reading happens in a separate
thread, so the error does not reach whoever launched the process: the call returns
normally, declaring that the engine said nothing. The arena score would not be
read, and the incident would resurface later, disguised as an error of a
completely different nature and far from its cause.

Verified by building the case on purpose:

| reading | result |
|---|---|
| system default encoding | **no text**, and no error reported |
| universal encoding (UTF-8), replacing unreadable characters | text intact, arena outcome readable |

**Fix**: the encoding is declared explicitly, and characters that cannot be
interpreted are replaced instead of interrupting the reading. An unreadable
character in a diagnostic message is not a good reason to throw away a whole
arena comparison.

**The lesson on method**: the six previous defects were discovered because they
produced a wrong number, and a wrong number sooner or later sounds off. This one
produces none — and a missing result is easier to blame on any other cause than on
itself.

## 6. Implementation correctness checks

They show that the results rest on an engine that is verified, not only fast.

| check | outcome |
|---|---|
| position count at increasing depth (perft) | matches the reference |
| position encoding | matching checksums between the two languages |
| C++ neural engine against the reference | maximum difference **0.000e+00** on logits, probabilities and values |
| negative control of the same check | a wrong output would give 7.14e−02, **714 times the threshold** |
| multi-thread concurrency | no interference, no deadlock, batching active |
| reading back the dataset produced by the engine | round-trip passed |
| compatibility of the rules with the external engine | **5 positions out of 5**, including the criterion that tells two chains apart by the order in which they meet a king (`tools/rules_crosscheck.py`) |
| bridge to the external engine | **120 positions out of 120** with the same set of legal moves, 24 with mandatory captures (`tools/kingsrow.py --validate`) |
| the whole chain on a second operating system | **9 suites out of 9** passed, complete cycles carried to the end |

### Portability

The chain was run in full on both operating systems used in the project: that of
the development machine and that of the compute machine. Neither run required
changes to the code.

It is worth recording **a known limit, not solved at the root**: on the
development system, parallel self-play on the CPU with the default number of
processes (one per core minus one, that is fifteen on that machine) can stop the
run with a native crash during training — not a language error, hence not
catchable. The point where it happens changes every time, which indicates memory
corruption and not a localized logic error. Remedies tried without effect:
limiting the threads of the compute library, and the environment variable that
usually resolves conflicts between parallelization libraries. Remedy **verified
effective**: reducing the number of processes to four, with which the same
configuration that used to stop reaches the end.

It does not affect production runs, and the reason is not the operating system but
the compute path: **with the compiled engine the process pool is not even
created**, so the crash has no way to show. Verified by reading the log of a real
run, which explicitly declares that it started neither the pool nor the inference
server because the engine covers both self-play and arena.

The clarification is needed because an earlier draft attributed the immunity to
production running *on the other system*. That is no longer the case: the
definitive run ran on Windows with a graphics card.

**How much the two platforms are really the same thing**, measured instead of
assumed. There is not a single branch conditioned on the operating system in the
code that *decides* something — conductor, search, model, training, pool,
inference server; the only one in the Python code is the exclusive lock on the
weights folder (`runlock.py`, with `fcntl` on Linux and `msvcrt` on Windows), which
decides nothing about play. In the C++ engine the only conditional compilation
concerns the presence of LibTorch, not the system. The remaining differences all lie
in the tools around the chain: closing a process tree, the process cap of the core
measurement, the launchers. None of these runs during a run.

On reproducibility, two distinct levels:

| comparison | outcome |
|---|---|
| same machine, same configuration | samples **bit-for-bit identical** |
| same machine, different number of threads | samples **bit-for-bit identical** |
| different machines or compilers | equivalent **within tolerance**, not identical |

The second case deserves attention because it was not a given: every game has its
own seed and its own search, so the number of threads changes only *how* the
games are distributed over the processors, not what they produce. It is a pure
performance lever — unlike leaf batching, which changes the search itself.

The third case is not a defect but a property of floating point: different
compilers vectorize differently, and on Ampere and later cards convolutions use
TF32, with ten bits of mantissa instead of twenty-three. What the project
guarantees is equivalence within tolerance, verified by the parity check between
the engine and PyTorch (1e-4 required, 0.000e+00 measured) and by the encoding
checksums, the latter exact.

stronger models resemble each other more, so telling them apart costs more. Over

| arenas | cycles | promotions |
|---|---|---|

| **reaching the cap of 400 games** | **37** | **2 (5%)** |

Thirty-seven times out of a hundred, with scores between 0.50 and 0.55. That is
**14,800 games out of 27,712 arena games in total, 53%** — more than half of the
time of the most expensive phase of the cycle, spent in comparisons that do not
conclude. The mean arena score of the whole run is **0.546**, with 45 promotions
over 100 cycles (run B: 0.528, with 64 promotions over 200).

**The duration of a match follows its indecision.** Across the cycles, the arena
time and the distance of the arena score from parity have a correlation of
**−0.59** in run A and **−0.62** in run B: the closer the two models, the longer
the match.

It is the figure that moves the first place among the things to tune. It is not
the number of distinct positions, nor the search budget, nor batching: it is that
**half of the decision effort is spent on not deciding**, and that the promotions
that come out of it cluster just above parity — exactly the condition section 4.6
describes as a walk rather than a climb.

**The unit cost depends on the path, not on the opponent.** An arena game, which
goes through the compiled multi-threaded engine, costs **5.3 seconds**; a game
against alpha-beta, which goes through the sequential interpreted path, costs tens
of seconds. A *fixed* opponent — for example a frozen champion, which the project
already saves at every generation — is an absolute reference just as much as
alpha-beta, but is played through the fast path.

**The arena is penalized by batching, and it is the most expensive phase.** The two
networks facing each other are different, so they cannot end up in the same batch:
the engine uses **two separate hubs**, and the card has to do two passes instead of
one, each on a smaller batch. Measured at eight threads: self-play 15.6 positions
per batch, arena 11.0 and 10.8.

The consequence on time, measured at equal threads and simulations: the arena costs
**1.35 times** a self-play game on the processor. On the data of the real run with
a graphics card the ratio is much worse — 1.43 s per self-play game against 5.47 per
arena game, that is **3.8 times** — consistent with the fact that on the card the
size of the batches matters much more.

The **threads** alone do not recover the gap: from 8 to 16 the batch goes from 10.7
to 10.8, because what limits the requests in flight are the games of the block,
not the threads. The lever that acts on the batch is **leaf batching**: raising it
from 8 to 32 doubles the arena batch, from 10.9 to 22.4.

**There is a structural ceiling, and it can be computed.** In the engine each thread
plays one game at a time and stays blocked on its own request until the answer
comes back: the requests in flight are therefore at most as many as the threads,
and since only the side to move is queried, each hub sees about half of them. The
maximum batch per hub is `(threads / 2) × leaves`. Measured at 8 threads, with a
block of 24, the real batching stops at about **a third** of that ceiling:

| leaves | batch per hub | ceiling | share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 11-12 | 32 | 35-39% |
| 16 | 21-23 | 64 | 33-35% |
| 32 | 35-41 | 128 | 28-32% |

The two causes can be separated by looking at the ratio between batch and leaves,
that is, how many requests end up merged: at 4 threads it is 0.81, **less than
one**, which shows that the search cannot gather all the leaves it asks for; at 8
threads it is about 1.3 out of 4 possible. Both causes therefore count — requests
smaller than asked and staggered arrivals — and the residual margin of a perfect
merge is about 2.5 times, no more.

**On TIME, however, these measurements do not conclude, and that is a result in
itself.** Repeating the very same command on the development machine, the same
configuration at 8 leaves gave 58.8, then 60.7, then 79.6 ms per ply: the
dispersion between repetitions **exceeds the differences between configurations**,
which are worth 10-15%. In one round the minimum fell at 32 leaves, in another at
8, and in a third 16 leaves came out the worst of the four. A first draft of this
section reported an interior maximum and a saturation of the gain: they were noise,
and are withdrawn.

Two causes contribute, both removed or declared. The fallback network of the bench
was random and **unseeded**, so every launch played different games — 129 mean
plies in one round, 101 in another; it is now seeded. And the number of games per
combination, twenty-four, is too small for 10% differences to emerge. The bench now
repeats each combination, reports the median with its dispersion, and when the
intervals of the first two overlap it says so instead of naming a winner.

The fact remains that the development machine is not the one the run runs on. A
tuning of time makes sense only where the graphics card is, because it is precisely
the ratio between network work and tree work — that is, what decides which lever
pays — that changes between processor and GPU. What can be taken away from these
measurements is the STRUCTURAL part, which does not depend on the machine: the
ceiling, the one-third share, and the 2.5 margin.

The block is not a free parameter anyway — the conductor derives it from the
threads — so a tuning that chose it at will would measure a configuration that
does not exist.

#### Leaf batching costs playing strength, and a lot of it

Up to this point the section treated batching as a pure performance choice, and an
earlier draft went as far as calling it **safe** because it does not change *what*
the engine plays. **That is false**, and it was measured.

More leaves per batch means fewer waves: with 400 simulations per move, 8 leaves
give 50 occasions for the tree to react to its own results, 64 give six. To gather
many leaves at once, the virtual loss forces the search onto branches it would not
have chosen.

Direct measurement: **the same trained network on both sides**, 400 simulations,
the only difference being the batching. Side A always uses 8 leaves (the engine's
`--mcts-batch-b` option; `results/series/batch_speed_strength.csv`):

| comparison | points for A | Elo disadvantage of B | W-D-L | games |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8 against 16 | 0.719 | −163 | 25-19-4 | 48 |
| 8 against 32 | 0.802 | −243 | 29-19-0 | 48 |
| 8 against 64 | 0.844 | −293 | 33-15-0 | 48 |

Between 32 and 64 the batched side does not win **a single game** out of 96. There
is no convenient intermediate value: the very first doubling, from 8 to 16, costs
163 Elo.

**The mechanism is specific to this game.** Italian draughts has 5.84 legal moves on
average (measured over 6,498 positions played by a trained network, median 6,
maximum 16). Gathering 64 leaves in a tree where every node has six children means
visiting almost all of it indiscriminately. In chess, with a branching factor
around 35, the same batch distorts incomparably less: **batching is the more
harmful the narrower the game**, and it is a result worth reporting because it
contradicts the intuition that batching is an implementation detail.

**Why no earlier measurement had detected it.** The promotion arena makes both
sides play with the *same* batching: the degradation applies to both, cancels out,
and the score stays at 0.5. The instrument that should have noticed was blind by
construction. The measurement reported here was possible only after adding to the
engine the option of giving the two sides different batching.

**Operational consequence.** The two performance levers are not equivalent and must
be treated separately:

| lever | effect on play | effect on time |
|---|---|---|
| **threads** | none: they change how many games run in parallel | 66.6 → 42.6 hours |
| **leaves** | −163 / −243 / −293 Elo | 42.6 → 20.7 / 15.7 hours |

The threads can be raised without a second thought. The leaves cannot: the speed
gain is real, but it is paid for by having the training data generated, for the
whole run, by a weaker player. The project's default is 8.

**And does the default itself cost something?** The measurements above compare other
values *with* 8: they say how much it costs to move away from it, not whether 8 is
already too much. If even 8 leaves lost against a finer search, the data of this
document would come from a player weaker than necessary, and the conclusion would be
"lower it", not "do not raise it". The check:

| comparison | points for A (4 leaves) | 95% interval | W-D-L | games |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 against 8 | 0.531 | 0.442 – 0.621 | 11-29-8 | 48 |

**No detectable difference**: 8 sits at **−22 Elo** relative to 4, with an interval
of **[−86, +41]** that amply contains zero. Forty-eight games do not prove equality,
but they rule out an effect larger than about **±60 Elo** — while the first step up,
from 8 to 16, costs 163.

The games are **48 distinct out of 48**: no repetition, so the result is not
inflated by counting the same game several times — the defect §5.1 describes for
the old arena.

> *History of the measurement, because it explains a correction*. A first version
> ran on **16 games at four threads**, the only row of the table not under the
> conditions of the others, and gave 0.469 for the 4-leaf side, with W-D-L 1-13-2
> and an interval of [−99, +54] Elo. Redone on 48 games at eight threads, the point
> estimate **changed sign** — from 0.469 to 0.531 — while staying indistinguishable
> from parity in both cases. It is the expected behavior of a value that oscillates
> around zero, and it serves as a reminder: on this row the sign of the point
> estimate carries no information.
>
> The interval narrowed **less than expected**, from ±76 to ±63 Elo instead of the
> hoped-for ±44, because the larger sample turned out to be much richer in decisive
> games — 19 out of 48, against 3 out of 16 — so the variance per game doubled. Part
> of the gain of quadrupling the games was eaten by an intrinsically noisier sample.
>
> The seed of the first run had not been kept: the current one uses **31337**,
> declared in the script.

The cost of batching is therefore **concentrated entirely above 8**, and the
project's default value falls exactly on the knee of the curve:

| leaves | 4 | **8** | 16 | 32 | 64 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elo relative to 8 | ≈ 0 | **—** | −163 | −243 | −293 |

It is a case in which the default was well chosen, but nobody knew: the choice had
been made for performance reasons, and this is the first measurement that checks
its consequence on play.

**The estimate against the actual figure.** The definitive run makes it possible to
check the benchmark on a real, complete case:

| | |
|---|---|
| estimated before starting | 43 hours |
| **measured** | **31.0 hours** |
| mean cycle predicted | 25.6 min |
| actual mean cycle | 18.6 min |
| longest cycle | 44.7 min |

The estimate is 39% too high, and the error has an identifiable cause: the benchmark
projects onto the **155 plies of the steady state**, while the run started from 120
and reached 159 only at the end. For much of the run the games were shorter than
assumed, hence cheaper. It is the same mechanism the benchmark's documentation
declares as its main source of error — with the opposite sign to the feared one:
one fears underestimating by measuring an immature network, and here the estimate
came out too high by projecting onto the final length.

The error is in the preferable direction — whoever receives the estimate does not
find the run longer than predicted — but it remains a 39% error on a forecast that
looked precise.

**Declared cores and available cores.** On rented machines the number of processors
the system declares is not the number that can be used: the machine is shared, and
a quota limits how much each guest gets. Measuring the real capacity — saturating
the machine with more processes than expected and counting the work actually done
— machines that declared many more gave **between 25 and 43 effective cores**, with
variations from one machine to another under the same commercial description.

It is operational information, not a result: it does, however, explain why the
duration of a cycle varies noticeably from one run to another, and why sizing the
parallelism on the declared number leads to asking the machine for three times what
it can give.

**What giving up the compiled engine and the graphics card costs.** On the
development machine, where at the time of this measurement the compiled engine did
not yet have the real network, and the interpreted chain on the CPU alone was
therefore used:

| | |
|---|---|
| time per self-play game (400 simulations) | ~173 s |
| fixed cost per cycle (training, arena, startup) | ~1,026 s |
| complete cycle, at the production configuration (400 games) | **~19.5 hours** |

Against the **~6 minutes** of the same cycle on the compute machine of the first
table: a ratio of about **195 to 1**. A hundred cycles would go from just over ten
hours to about **eighty-one days**.

Two caveats make the number useful instead of striking. The estimate extrapolates
from just two points (2 and 8 games per cycle), so it is an order of magnitude, not
a fine measurement. And it is not a clean comparison between processor and graphics
card: on one side there is the interpreted chain limited to four processes — a cap
imposed by the crash described above, not a performance choice — on the other the
compiled engine with dozens of threads. It measures **what each machine can actually
do**, which is the operational question, and on its own it justifies the choice of
writing the engine in a compiled language instead of optimizing the interpreted one.

## 7. Final configuration

| parameter | value | on what basis |
|---|---|---|
| exploration constant | 1.5 | measured (§2.1) |
| simulations per move | 400 | the diagnosis rules out the budget as the constraint (§3.4) |
| games per cycle | 400 | |
| replay window | 600,000 samples (~10 cycles) | measurement of the gap between seen and new data (§2.4) |
| value discount | 0.99 | measured on behavior (§2.3) |
| sampled plies in the arena | 10 | fix of defect §5.1 |
| architecture | 96 channels, 6 blocks, 1.13 M parameters | capacity sufficient by construction (§2.4) |
| epochs per cycle | 2 | beyond 10 the value head overfits |
| **leaf batching** | **8** | **measured: 16 costs 163 Elo, 32 costs 243 (§6)** |
| cycles | 100 | run A; run B's 200 cycles buy a modest but significant +67 Elo (§4.8) |

The first eight rows had already been established; the last two were settled by
measuring. For batching, the value in use turned out to be the right one for
reasons nobody had checked before; for the cycles, the measurement says that more
of them buy a little more strength (§4.8).

**On batching**: it was a performance parameter, chosen to make the graphics card
work better. It turned out to affect **playing strength**, and heavily. The value 8
falls on the knee of the curve: below it nothing is gained (4 against 8 is
indistinguishable), above it much is lost at once. It must be treated as a
hyperparameter of the model, not as a system knob.

**On the cycles**: the number is not only the stopping condition, it also governs
the cosine decay of the learning rate along the run. Choosing it is therefore
irreversible — a run started for a hundred cycles and then extended to two hundred
would see the learning rate **rise** by a factor of 5.4 at cycle 101, instead of
continuing its descent.

### Performance-only parameters, which do not touch play

Kept apart from the table above on purpose: changing them does not alter the samples
produced, verified by comparing them bit for bit.

| parameter | value | note |
|---|---|---|
| engine threads | ¾ of the logical processors (set by `run.ps1`) | 24 out of 32; measured −46% on the cost per ply relative to 13 |
| device | chosen by probing the card | the old CPU default cost orders of magnitude, silently |
| SPRT block | derived from the threads | not free: the conductor computes it |

The distinction between the two tables is itself a result: **threads change how many
games are played in parallel, batching changes how each one is played**. Both looked
like performance knobs, and one of the two was not.

## 9. The implemented draw rule is not the official one

**Checked against the FID rules** (Regolamento Ufficiale di Gioco della Dama
Italiana, Chapter I, articles 9 and 10). It is the only entry of this collection that
concerns not a measurement but the **definition of the game studied**: if the rule
diverges, the results concern a draughts variant slightly different from the Italian
one.

### What the rules say

| article | content |
|---|---|
| 10.1 | there must be **at least one king for each player** |
| 10.2 | the count is **requested by a player** or imposed by the referee |
| 10.4 | draw after **40 king moves of the counted player**, if the number of pieces has not changed |
| 10.5 | the count is **cancelled** if either player **moves a man**, or if the number of pieces changes |

### What the implementation does

Automatic draw after **80 consecutive plies without a capture or a promotion**, with
no condition on material.

### The four differences

1. **Applicability condition**: the rules require a king per side, the
   implementation sets no condition.
2. **What is counted**: the rules count the moves of a single player, the
   implementation the plies of both.
3. **What resets the count** — *the difference that matters*: the rules reset it at
   every **man move** by either player; the implementation only on a capture or a
   promotion, and keeps counting while the men move.
4. **Activation**: the rules require a request, the implementation is automatic. It
   is a necessary adaptation for self-play, but it must be said.

> *A note that resolves an apparent ambiguity between sources*: two federation
> websites disagree on whether the 40 are **king** moves or any moves. The
> distinction has no effect: since any man move resets the count (10.5), between
> one reset and the next **only kings** move, and the two readings coincide.

### How much it diverges, measured

Twelve self-play games with a trained network, diversified openings, counting the
implemented and the official criterion in parallel (`tools/draw_rule_check.py`):

| | |
|---|---|
| games closed by the implemented rule | **8 of 12** |
| of these, games in which the official rule would also have triggered | **6 of 8** |
| **draws the rules would NOT grant** | **2 of 8 (25%)** |

In the two diverging games the official count stood at 37 and 18 out of 40: the game
would have continued. The direction of the error is therefore **always the same** —
the implementation closes earlier than the rules allow — because the official count
resets more often and can never trigger before the implemented one.

**Reach**: §1 reports that ~35% of games end by this limit. If a quarter of those are
premature, that is about **9% of all games** that the rules would have let continue.
It is game material the system does not see: endgames that would have had an outcome
and get labelled as draws.

*Caution*: twelve games with a single network are a small sample, and the share
depends on the style of the network (§4.1 measures it again on the definitive run).
The direction of the error, however, does not depend on the sample: it follows from
the rule.

### Why it was not corrected

Changing the criterion changes the game, and therefore:

- it would invalidate the comparison with **all** the previous runs, which played with
  the current rule;
- it would require the same change **in the C++ engine**, otherwise the two paths
  would play different games — an inconsistency worse than the imprecise rule;
- it needs more state than the current simple counter (who the counted player is, and
  the distinction between man moves and king moves).

It is a design decision, not a fix: it must be taken knowing that it costs
comparability with the data already collected.

#### The rule is wired into the network, not only into the adjudication

There is a fourth obstacle, found while trying to replay with the official count the
endgames of the games against the external engine (§4.1), and it is the most binding
because it concerns not the code but the **trained model**.

Among the input planes of the network there is one that encodes how far away the draw
is: the no-progress counter divided by the threshold, clipped at one. The network has
therefore learned with that denominator, and uses that information to decide — it
knows that at some point the game will end in a draw, and plays accordingly.

Two practical consequences follow.

**The endgames cannot be replayed beyond the current threshold.** Past the eightieth
ply that plane stays pinned at its maximum value, and the network finds itself in a
state it never met in training: the result of the replay would partly measure
out-of-distribution behavior, not the rules. A correction after the fact is therefore
**ruled out**, and the only practicable way to estimate the effect of the rule on the
results already collected is statistical — measuring the divergence rate on new games
and applying it.

**And adopting the official rule requires retraining.** It is not a peripheral change
that can be applied to an existing network: it changes the meaning of one of the
inputs, so the model must be rebuilt from scratch. The cost of the fix is not writing
the count, which is little: it is a complete run.

It is worth recording as a general observation, because it is counterintuitive: in
this architecture **an adjudication rule is not a convention external to the game, it
is an input of the model**. Every choice of the kind — the draw threshold, the ply
cap, adjudication by material in self-play — enters the weights and stays there.
